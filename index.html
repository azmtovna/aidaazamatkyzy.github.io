Two kinds of period pain

“Dysmenorrhoea” is the medical word for painful periods. Doctors split it in two — and the split decides what happens next.

No underlying disease

Cramps come from prostaglandins, substances that make the uterus contract. It usually begins within a year or so of the first period, lasts a couple of days, and often eases with age. Heat, movement and anti-inflammatory painkillers are the usual tools.

A condition is causing it

Endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids or pelvic infection can sit behind the pain. It more often appears in the 30s and 40s, and the two types can overlap. Clues: pain that starts days before bleeding, lasts longer, grows worse over time or continues after the period; pain during sex; bleeding between periods; very heavy bleeding.

Why it matters: roughly one woman in five has pain strong enough to disrupt daily life. Painkillers can quiet the signal while the cause stays untreated.

Is painful normal?

Some cramping is normal: the uterus contracts to shed its lining. But common is not the same as normal — pain that stops you studying, working or moving is not something you are supposed to simply endure.

See a doctor if

  • pain stops you doing normal things
  • it is getting worse, or lasts longer than before
  • bleeding is very heavy, with tiredness or dizziness
  • pain during sex, or bleeding between periods
  • your usual painkiller stops working

What it can lead to

  • iron-deficiency anaemia from heavy bleeding
  • years of delay before a diagnosis (7–10 years is often cited for endometriosis)
  • trouble conceiving in some conditions
  • chronic pain, fatigue, lost study and work — the “full desk” loss

The Dutch data says most women show up anyway. That is exactly why nobody sees it.

What could be behind the pain

Three conditions share symptoms and are easily confused — so diagnosis matters, because treatment differs.

Tissue outside the womb

Tissue like the womb lining grows in the pelvis, on the ovaries or the bowel. Pain may start before and continue after bleeding, and can come with pain during sex or bowel movements. It can affect fertility. WHO: about 10% of women of reproductive age, around 190 million. Confirming it often needs imaging and sometimes keyhole surgery (laparoscopy).

Tissue inside the muscle wall

The same kind of tissue grows into the uterine muscle. The uterus can enlarge and feel tender; periods are heavy and long, cramps severe. Usually found with ultrasound or MRI. It can exist together with endometriosis.

Benign lumps in the muscle

Non-cancerous growths. Many people have none of the symptoms; others get heavy or irregular bleeding and a feeling of pressure or fullness. The uterus can feel lumpy rather than evenly enlarged.

Where the tissue sits is the difference. It decides the scan, the treatment and the questions to ask a doctor.

Is menopause the end?

The last period is not a finish line. It is the point where a long hormonal change becomes visible.

Perimenopause

Often starts in the 40s and can last years. Estrogen rises and falls unpredictably: irregular cycles, hot flushes, poor sleep, mood changes. Periods may get heavier or more erratic before they stop.

Menopause

Defined looking back: twelve months without a period. The average age is about 51. Earlier menopause means a longer time without estrogen.

Postmenopause

Low estrogen speeds up bone loss, fastest in roughly the first ten years after the last period. Cardiovascular risk rises. Vaginal dryness and painful sex may persist or worsen. And any bleeding after menopause should be checked by a doctor.

Periods end. The hormonal story does not. The shift does not disappear — it changes name, and the silence around it often continues.
